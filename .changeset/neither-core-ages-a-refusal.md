Two native behaviour fixes, both of them the same subject: what a stored record is still worth. `PolicyEvaluator.kt` and `PolicyEvaluator.swift` now evaluate a decision the way `evaluate.ts` in `@c15t/core` does on three axes that were graded only wherever a committed scenario happened to reach.

Out of a policy's scope the answer comes from `scopeMode` alone and never from the model, which is the first branch of `defaultPermission`: a permissive rule allows a category it does not govern whether the rule says `opt-in`, `iab`, `opt-out` or `none`, and a strict rule refuses it under all four. Both cores consulted the model there instead, so an app under a rule that governs two of four categories showed the other two as denied with nothing behind it, and a category the subject had actively refused came back allowed under `opt-out`.

A refusal does not age. `explicit-denial` is now read off the raw stored decision whatever that receipt's authority says, so a `false` still restricts after the choice lapses and after the policy fingerprint moves, which is what `collectRestrictions` does. The Kotlin core declared `strict-scope`, `gpc` and `opt-out-directive` and no denial reason at all, so it gains one, and it gains the kernel's ordering with it: `["explicit-denial", "strict-scope"]` is the pair a refusal outside a strict scope produces, not a set. The prompt also says why it is owed now. A refusal anywhere in the choice scope silences an automatic prompt first, then nothing recorded at all, then a basis the current policy no longer covers, then a grant that lapsed; that is `deriveChoiceRequirement` in order, and the answer it makes reachable is `expired`.

Values move, keys do not. The snapshot's `prompt.reason` was already on the contract and the Swift core already wrote it; the Android core derived it from `purpose`, which collapses a lapsed receipt and a moved fingerprint into `policy-changed`, and now copies what its own evaluator found, so a device whose grant has aged out reports `expired`. `consentPermissions` answers differently for any category a permissive rule leaves ungoverned, and `restrictions` gains `explicit-denial` on both platforms for every stored `false`. Under a `choice` rule only the choice obligation reaches the wire, because that is the only kind the kernel names there. The stored envelope keeps its shape and the save body gains no key. Eleven `evaluation-*` protocol fixtures cover the axes that had none: a scope narrower than the full four, once permissive and once strict, a refusal paired with an expired receipt and with a policy revision the record predates, and an `iab` rule held to the same table as `opt-in`. Both cores claim all of them.
